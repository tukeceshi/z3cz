# Camera-language decision guide

Use this guide to choose camera geometry and movement from narrative intent. These are tendencies, not automatic formulas; performance, blocking, production design, and context can reverse an effect.

## Viewpoint and power

| Choice | Typical viewer relationship | Strong uses | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eye level | Equality, presence, observation without overt judgment | dialogue, intimacy, restrained emotion, realism | Flatness if depth, blocking, or focal length is unspecified |
| High angle | Subject placed inside a larger order | vulnerability, surveillance, isolation, spatial explanation | “High angle” alone does not state height or purpose |
| Vertical overhead | Diagrammatic space, enclosure, ritual, loss of agency | choreography, bodies/objects in a pattern, entrapment | Faces and eye-lines may become unreadable |
| Low angle | Increased visual weight and dominance | entrance, threat, awe, monumentality | Wide lenses near faces can distort anatomy |
| Dutch angle | Destabilized horizon | rupture, intoxication, panic, moral imbalance | Becomes decorative if nothing motivates the tilt |
| OTS / dirty frame | Relational tension and subjective proximity | dialogue, confrontation, conceal/reveal | Keep eye-lines and shoulder ownership consistent |
| POV | Viewer inherits a character's information limits | discovery, danger, embodiment | Do not show information the character cannot see |
| Obstructed / voyeur view | Distance, secrecy, surveillance | suspense, social separation | Obstruction must not cover the essential action |

Always make geometry concrete: camera height, subject-relative direction, approximate distance, tilt, and intended subject scale.

## Lens and space

- **14–24 mm ultra-wide / wide:** expands depth, strengthens converging lines, increases presence and speed near camera. Useful for spatial immersion, pursuit, low-angle entrances, and confined environments. Keep faces away from edges unless distortion is intentional.
- **28–40 mm moderate wide:** keeps environment active while preserving a natural human presence. Useful for walk-and-talks, handheld following, and full-body staging.
- **45–58 mm normal:** neutral perspective at ordinary working distances. Useful for restrained observation and dialogue.
- **65–100 mm short telephoto:** isolates faces, narrows the field, and calms background movement. Useful for pressure, withheld observation, and performance detail.
- **135–200 mm telephoto:** compresses layers and produces remote surveillance or dense urban depth. Requires real camera distance and careful tracking.
- **Macro / extreme close focus:** turns material detail into story information; specify focus target and scale.

Perspective comes from camera distance; focal length controls framing from that distance. When precision matters, describe both rather than claiming the lens alone creates perspective.

## Framing and depth

Specify the subject scale only as tightly as needed: extreme wide, wide/full body, medium, medium close-up, close-up, extreme close-up. Add composition when it carries meaning:

- Foreground occlusion can create depth, surveillance, or discovery.
- Negative space can anticipate an entrance, imply absence, or place pressure on a subject.
- Centered symmetry can imply order, ritual, control, or entrapment.
- Converging architecture can direct attention and amplify approach.
- Telephoto layer compression can connect a subject emotionally to distant traffic, crowds, or lights.
- Wide-angle separation can make distances and movement legible.

State eye-line, lead room, headroom, screen direction, and important background reveal when continuity depends on them.

## Camera movement by dramatic function

| Movement | Observable instruction | Typical function |
|---|---|---|
| Pan / tilt | Rotate from a fixed camera position | reveal, follow, redirect attention |
| Dolly in / push in | Translate toward subject | growing realization, pressure, commitment |
| Dolly out / pull back | Translate away | isolation, context reveal, emotional withdrawal |
| Truck / lateral track | Move sideways | compare layers, follow travel, reveal obstacles |
| Follow / lead | Maintain relationship in front of or behind subject | momentum, companionship, pursuit |
| Arc / orbit | Move around subject on a defined arc | changing allegiance, revelation, power shift |
| Crane / jib | Change height and often distance | scale reveal, transition between human and environment |
| Handheld | Operator-borne micro-movement with stated intensity | immediacy, instability, embodied observation |
| Locked-off | No pan, tilt, translation, zoom, shake, or drift | tension, scrutiny, aftermath, contrast |
| Zoom | Change focal length without camera translation | perceptual emphasis or stylized distance change |
| Dolly zoom | Dolly and counter-zoom while holding subject size | spatial disorientation; use sparingly and specify direction |
| Rack focus | Shift focus between named planes | transfer attention or reveal information; not camera movement |

Avoid vague “dynamic camera.” Name the rig or physical behavior only when it affects motion: tripod, shoulder, gimbal, dolly, crane, vehicle mount, drone, or body-mounted.

## Beat-bound motion grammar

For a continuous shot, define:

1. **Start state:** exact position, angle, lens, framing, subject pose/action, and spatial anchor.
2. **Trigger:** the action, line, glance, impact, or reveal that initiates camera motion.
3. **Move:** path, direction, height change, speed and easing, stabilization character, and whether lens/focus changes.
4. **Framing rule:** what the operator preserves or deliberately changes during the move.
5. **End state:** exact final position, framing, new information, and hold duration or transition.

Write simultaneous relationships explicitly: “As the actor turns 180°, the camera rises from knee height to shoulder height, ending behind the actor.” Write later actions as separate beats.

## Sequence continuity

- Establish geography before rapid coverage unless disorientation is intentional.
- Preserve the 180-degree axis, eye-lines, travel direction, prop hand, actor position, and light direction across cuts.
- Cross the axis through visible camera movement, a neutral-on-axis shot, or an intentional rupture.
- Vary shot size or angle enough to justify a cut; avoid near-identical jump cuts unless desired.
- Give each cut a motivation: action, look, sound, information reveal, emotional reversal, or rhythmic punctuation.
- Use contrast deliberately: moving to locked, wide to close, objective to subjective, stable to unstable.
